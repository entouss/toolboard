School Tools

Curriculum Explorer

Plan four years of classes in about fifteen minutes.

A course catalog is a list. A schedule is a plan. Turning one into the other means holding prerequisite chains, credit minimums and which year each course is open to all in your head at once — which is why it usually takes an afternoon and a legal pad. Drag courses onto a grid instead, and let the tool do the checking.

Try it with an example catalog 280 courses · nothing to install Open Toolboard

That link opens Toolboard, installs the School Tools plugin, adds a Curriculum Explorer and loads the catalog into it. Everything stays in your browser — no account, no upload, nothing sent anywhere.

What it checks for you

Prerequisites
Every course you place is checked against what has to come first — and whether that course finishes early enough. A prerequisite tree shows the whole chain behind any course, so you can see how deep it goes before you commit to it.
Requirements
Credits are counted against each requirement in your catalog as you plan, including courses that satisfy two at once. Open any requirement to see exactly which courses are making up the number.
When it is offered
Some courses run one semester only, some only in summer, some only for juniors and seniors. Put one where it cannot go and the tool says so, rather than letting you find out at registration.

How to build a plan in fifteen minutes

The order matters more than the speed. Working outwards from the courses with the least freedom leaves the easy choices for last, when the shape of the plan is already settled.

  1. Tick off what is already done Anything taken before the plan starts counts towards graduation without taking a seat in it. Select a course and press Already met. Students often arrive with high school credit earned in middle school — Algebra 1 and a world language are the usual ones.
  2. Decide the yearly course load Six or seven credits a year, whatever your school runs. Each year in the grid shows its own credit total, so you will see immediately when one is overloaded.
  3. Place the courses that have no alternative The required sequences, one per year, straight down the grid. This is most of the plan and it takes about a minute. English 1, 2, 3, 4 in grades 9 through 12.
  4. Work the longest chain backwards Find the course you most want to reach, put it in the final year, then place each of its prerequisites in the year before. The Tree tab shows the chain if you are not sure how deep it runs. Calculus in grade 12 means Pre-Calculus in 11, Algebra II in 10 and Geometry in 9 — so Algebra 1 has to have been taken already.
  5. Balance the years Drag courses between years until the credit totals even out. Anything that lands where it cannot go says so as you drop it.
  6. Fill the gaps with electives Filter the catalog by department or requirement to find courses that count for something you are still short on, rather than picking blind.
  7. Clear the Issues tab Every prerequisite conflict, ordering problem and unmet requirement is listed in one place. Click one to jump to the course it concerns. When the tab is empty, the plan holds together.
  8. Take it to your counselor Save the plan as a PNG, or print the catalog as a PDF, and go through it with someone who knows this year's master schedule. A plan that validates is still a plan — the school decides what actually runs.

Recording grades

A plan says what was taken. Grades say how it went, and together they are a transcript. Every course you place can carry a mark for each grading period and a final grade, and those roll up into a GPA per year, per school and across the whole career.

Marking periods
Taken from the school's own terms rather than assumed. Set how many marks each term carries — two gives a semester school four quarters a year, one gives it two semester grades, and a trimester school works the same way. Turn on exams and each term gets one, weighted however the school weights it.
Whatever scale it uses
Letters, letters with plus and minus, percentages, points out of 4.0 or 5.0, or numbers where 5 is best — or 1. If none of those is right, define your own as a list of grades and what each is worth. The tool needs the point values to be able to average anything at all.
Calculated, or yours
The final is the average of the marks by default, with the exam blended in at its weight. Type a final over the top and it stays — shown as entered rather than as arithmetic, so you can always tell which is which.
  1. Set the school's grading once Scale, marks per term, exams, weighting — in the JSON pane beside the rest of that school's setup. Each school has its own, because a middle school and a high school rarely mark the same way.
  2. Enter a report card in one pass The Grades tab lists every course you have placed, a column per marking period, grouped by year. A report card arrives with every course on it at once, and this is the screen for that. One course at a time is easier from the course itself: select it and its marks are under the details.
  3. Read it back off the grid Each card shows its final grade, and each year shows credits and GPA beside each other. The Whole career page adds a GPA per school and one for the career — pooled by points, so two schools on different scales still add up.

The GPA is credit-weighted — a half-credit course counts half — over the courses that have a grade. Turn on weighting and honors adds 0.5, AP and its equivalents 1.0, read off the level the catalog already carries. Where nothing carries credit at all, as in most middle schools, it falls back to a plain average rather than dividing by nothing. It is your arithmetic, not the school's: a transcript is the authority, and this is a well-checked estimate of it.

One student, several schools

A school career is not one document. Elementary, middle and high school each publish their own, and plenty of students attend two high schools. The Explorer holds all of them at once — a dropdown at the top switches between them, and picking one gives you the tool exactly as described above: that school's catalog, that school's plan, that school's graduation requirements. Nothing is merged and nothing is averaged.

  1. Add a school for each one attended Press + School, load its curriculum document, and set the grades the student was actually there for. The grades matter when two schools both publish a 9–12 catalog. Westhaven 9–10 and Ashford 11–12 gives each of them a two-year grid, which is what actually happened.
  2. Record the past, plan the future For schools already behind you, place what was taken. For the current one, plan forwards as normal.
  3. Tick what transferred Under Transferred in, every course recorded at an earlier school is offered against the current school's catalog. Ticking one marks that course as already met, so prerequisites and credit totals treat it exactly as they would anything else you had already done. This is where a high-school-credit course taken in middle school finally counts. GEM-7 (Algebra I Honors) at the middle school is matched to Algebra I at the high school — and flagged as a close match rather than asserted, because the titles are not the same and only you know whether the credit was accepted.
  4. Add credit for anything that does not match A school grants credit for courses it does not itself teach. Those have nothing to tick, so record them as their own line — a title, a credit value and the subject it counts towards — and the totals include them, listed underneath so the number stays traceable.
  5. Read the whole thing The last entry in the dropdown is Whole career: every school in the order attended, every year, what was taken in it and what it was worth. It is a record rather than a plan — open a school to change anything — and the PNG button saves it as one picture.
  6. Take it away as a spreadsheet CSV saves the same record as data: a row per course, with the school, the year, the term, the credits and the grade alongside the points behind it, so two schools marking on different scales still add up in a spreadsheet. It exports whichever view is showing — the whole career, or one school on its own. This is the shape worth holding beside the official transcript. The transcript is still the authority; a column of your own next to it is how you find the year where they disagree.

The catalogs stay separate on purpose. Two high schools in one district share course titles by the dozen — Geometry, Algebra II, Chemistry. Merged into a single document, a prerequisite naming "Geometry" would quietly resolve to whichever school's version happened to load first. Kept apart, each school's prerequisites can only ever mean that school's courses, and what crosses between them is the list you ticked yourself.

Building a catalog for your own school

The tool reads one JSON file. There is no library of schools behind it — you make the file once, and it is yours. Two other tools in the same toolbox exist for exactly that job: the Curriculum Builder writes the file, and the Curriculum Doctor tells you what is wrong with one.

Curriculum Builder School Tools

A structured editor over the catalog. Courses in a list you add to and edit in a form, with every prerequisite title resolved against the titles that actually exist — typed wrong, it says so and offers the spellings that would work. Subjects, pathways and the shape of the plan get their own sections, and the running tally at the top is the Doctor's own checks, applied while there is still someone at the keyboard.

Open the Builder Open it on an example
  1. Gather the source documents Your school's course catalog or selection card, as a PDF if you can get one. You will usually need more than that: the district's graduation requirements, and the rules for any special programme — AICE, AP Capstone, IB, a career academy.
  2. Set the shape before the courses In the Builder, start an empty curriculum and fill in Document, Planner and Subjects first: what the school is called, what years it runs, what terms divide them, and what it asks for to graduate. It is five minutes, and every course you add afterwards is checked against it.
  3. Type the first course of each sequence, then duplicate it Most of a catalog is sequences. English 1, 2, 3 and 4 differ by a word and a year, so type one and press Duplicate three times. The Subjects section counts, as you type, how many courses and how many credits could ever satisfy each requirement — so a subject named differently from the department it is meant to match says so immediately, rather than showing an empty bar in someone's plan a month later.
  4. Or convert it first, and open the result here Handing the documents and the schema to an AI assistant gets you most of a file in one go. Expect it to be wrong in specific ways: course numbers transposed, prerequisites flattened, whole sections skipped. Open what it produced in the Builder and fix it in place — nothing it wrote is lost, including the fields the form does not show.
  5. Work the tally down to nothing Open the count at the top of the Builder and it lists every finding by kind, with the reason each one matters. Press one to go to the course it is about. The Curriculum Doctor runs the same checks on their own, and writes a repair brief you can hand back to whatever produced the file.
  6. Save it, and plan against it Save writes the .json. Load that into the Curriculum Explorer, and the file you just built becomes four years of somebody's schooling.

If you would rather see the schema itself: in the Explorer press JSON, switch to Schema, and press Show the JSON. That is the full description of what these tools read, in the version that matches the tools you are running.

A catalog is a description, not an authority. Every file — the examples here included — is somebody's reading of a school document, and readings contain mistakes. Treat a finished plan as a well-checked draft to take to your counselor, never as a registration.
